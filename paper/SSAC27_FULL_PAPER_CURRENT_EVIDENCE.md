# When Should AI Defer? Uncertainty-Aware Video Review for Human-in-the-Loop Officiating in Competitive Taekwondo

**Keivan Shariatmadar¹, Ahmad Osman¹, Ramin Rey²**  
¹ htw saar – University of Applied Sciences, Saarbrücken, Germany  
² Austrian Taekwondo Federation (AUT), Austria

## Abstract

### Introduction
Video review in Taekwondo must resolve high-speed scoring actions from imperfect visual evidence, often under occlusion, rapid body rotation, and limited camera perspective. Accuracy alone is therefore an incomplete objective for AI-assisted officiating: a useful system should also identify when evidence is too weak for a confident recommendation. We evaluate FST.ai as a human-in-the-loop review framework and ask what can be concluded from its championship-derived candidate evidence without conflating human annotation confidence with model epistemic uncertainty.

### Methods
We audited 546 FST candidate clips from 74 recorded match identifiers. Each candidate had a human validation label (True Positive or False Positive), human confidence, difficulty, kick type, contact quality, and row-aligned visibility. Raw records were preserved; one exact duplicate match/clip entry was excluded from the canonical population, and two identifiers documented as the same bout were treated as one clustering unit. We report candidate-confirmation statistics with a delete-one-bout cluster jackknife interval and quantify dependence among annotation fields. Because the candidate table does not independently enumerate missed true events, recall and F1 are not estimated. A separate evaluation protocol requires future model uncertainty analysis to use numerical uncertainty generated by the frozen FST implementation and to evaluate selective prediction through risk-coverage.

### Results
The canonical population contains 545 candidate clips: 449 human-validated true events and 96 false candidates, yielding an 82.39% candidate-confirmation rate (bout-cluster jackknife 95% CI: 81.16%–83.61%). The audit revealed unusually strong annotation dependence: 535 of 546 raw records (97.99%) fall into four repeated joint patterns. Of 222 partially occluded candidates, 126 are TP-labelled and 96 FP-labelled, a descriptive candidate-confirmation fraction of 56.76%. All 96 FP-labelled candidates share the same Medium-confidence, Hard, uncertain-event, uncertain-contact, partial-occlusion annotation pattern. This concentrates the observed false candidates in difficult visual evidence, but the structural coupling prevents these human fields from being interpreted as independent uncertainty calibration.

### Conclusion
For AI-assisted officiating, the relevant question is not only whether the model can classify an action, but whether it knows when to defer. The audit establishes a reproducible evidence boundary for FST: candidate-only annotations support candidate-validation analysis, while full performance requires an independent event census that observes false negatives and frozen model outputs for risk-coverage analysis. The released de-identified data and evaluation code make this distinction auditable and preserve human authority for ambiguous cases.

**Keywords:** sports analytics; Taekwondo; video review; human-in-the-loop AI; epistemic uncertainty; selective prediction; abstention; computer vision; officiating

## 1. Introduction

Technology-assisted officiating is now embedded in many high-performance sports, but its value depends on more than whether an algorithm produces a label quickly. In a high-stakes review, the system must operate under incomplete evidence, make its limitations visible, and preserve a clear route back to human judgment. This is particularly important in combat sports, where decisive actions unfold in fractions of a second and may be partially hidden by body rotation, athlete overlap, camera geometry, or contact ambiguity.

Taekwondo is a useful test bed for this problem. Electronic Protector and Scoring Systems (PSS) automate parts of scoring, while Instant Video Replay (IVR) remains part of the adjudication process under World Taekwondo rules [1]. Head-kick situations are especially demanding because the decisive evidence may be brief, partially occluded, or mechanically unregistered. The governing rules and exact appeal pathways evolve over time; accordingly, an AI decision-support system must be designed around the current competition process rather than around a fixed historical replay workflow.

FST.ai was introduced as an AI-assisted framework for Taekwondo officiating, combining computer vision, pose/motion analysis, impact-related evidence, and edge inference for rapid review support [2]. FST.ai 2.0 expanded that line toward pose-based action recognition, explainability, and epistemic uncertainty using credal-set ideas [3]. The conceptual objective is not autonomous refereeing. It is a human-in-the-loop workflow in which AI supplies evidence and may recommend an action while the authorized official remains the final decision maker.

This distinction matters because a conventional accuracy-only evaluation can hide the cases that matter most operationally. A classifier may achieve a high aggregate rate while making its most consequential errors precisely on occluded, ambiguous, or distribution-shifted inputs. Selective classification addresses this by allowing a model to reject or abstain on uncertain cases, explicitly trading coverage for lower risk [4]. Related work on learning to defer and human-AI selective prediction further shows that the quality of a human-AI system depends on how deferral is designed and communicated, not only on the standalone model [5,6]. More broadly, uncertainty estimates themselves can degrade under dataset shift and should not be assumed reliable without direct evaluation [7].

Recent Taekwondo-specific research reinforces the relevance of this perspective. Zhang, Qu and Girard evaluated 241 video-review cases from the Paris 2024 Olympic Taekwondo competition and reported strong AI-referee agreement (Cohen's κ = 0.897), nine discrepant cases, and an approximately 81% reduction in review time. Notably, the discrepancies were concentrated in visually difficult cases such as minimal head contact and occlusion, and the authors argued for continued human oversight [8]. This finding is closely aligned with the practical motivation for uncertainty-aware FST review: the difficult tail of the evidence distribution is more consequential than the average case.

However, uncertainty-aware evaluation requires a clean separation between three different objects: **reference evidence**, **model output**, and **derived performance labels**. If a human annotator's confidence, difficulty rating, or visibility judgment is created with knowledge of whether a candidate is a false positive, those contextual variables cannot later be treated as independent validation of model uncertainty. Likewise, a table containing only model-proposed candidate events cannot establish recall unless an independent census of true events identifies missed detections.

The present study therefore focuses on the evidence layer itself. It audits the championship-derived FST candidate annotations, quantifies their structure, identifies duplication and provenance issues, establishes what the data can and cannot support, and formalizes the selective-prediction evaluation that should be used for the frozen FST implementation. The aim is deliberately narrower than claiming a new end-to-end FST accuracy number.

The research questions are:

**RQ1.** What empirical statements can be supported by the available championship-derived FST candidate annotations after an explicit provenance and duplicate audit?

**RQ2.** How are false-positive-labelled candidates distributed across the human annotations for confidence, difficulty, action type, contact quality, and visibility?

**RQ3.** Which parts of the annotation table are valid contextual evidence, and which must not be interpreted as FST epistemic uncertainty?

**RQ4.** What evaluation design is required to move from candidate validation to a defensible uncertainty-aware, human-in-the-loop officiating benchmark?

The main contribution is consequently not a single headline accuracy value. It is an auditable transition from a candidate-level evidence table to an evaluation architecture in which claims, denominators, dependence, uncertainty, and human deferral are explicitly defined.

### 1.1 Contributions

The study makes four concrete contributions that correspond directly to the evaluation demands of applied sports analytics.

**Evidence audit.** We reconstruct and release a de-identified candidate-level evidence layer, preserve the raw observations, identify exact duplication and match-identity conflicts, and create a canonical analysis population without silently rewriting the source record.

**Validity analysis.** We show quantitatively that the human confidence, difficulty, action ambiguity, contact ambiguity, visibility, and TP/FP fields are strongly coupled. This prevents an attractive but invalid interpretation of human annotation confidence as calibrated FST epistemic uncertainty.

**Decision-to-defer evaluation design.** We define an event-level benchmark that requires independently frozen ground truth and model outputs, bout-aware uncertainty intervals, deterministic event matching, and selective-prediction evaluation through coverage and retained risk. The design makes false negatives and human deferral observable rather than optimizing only the correctness of surfaced candidates.

**Reproducible research package.** We provide the de-identified evidence used for the present results, an evidence ledger, audit logs, analysis code, event-matching code, validation tools, a statistical analysis plan, a frozen-model export specification, and automated repository checks. This makes the present claims reproducible while keeping proprietary implementation assets and third-party footage outside the public package unless separately cleared.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Technology and AI in sports officiating

Technology-assisted officiating spans electronic scoring, line and goal systems, video-assistant systems, and increasingly computer-vision-based decision support. Recent reviews argue that AI in refereeing should be treated as a socio-technical system rather than as an isolated prediction problem, because adoption depends on transparency, workflow fit, responsibility, and human acceptance in addition to technical accuracy [9].

AI-powered video assistant systems in football and other team sports have been studied as mechanisms for reducing decision time and increasing consistency, although complex edge cases remain difficult [10]. Combat sports create additional challenges: high-speed articulated motion, frequent self- and mutual occlusion, rapid camera-relative pose changes, and scoring rules that depend on both action type and valid contact.

### 2.2 AI-assisted Taekwondo video review

FST.ai introduced an AI-assisted Taekwondo officiating pipeline aimed especially at head-kick review [2]. The framework combines visual perception and action-related evidence with low-latency inference. FST.ai 2.0 broadened the concept to explainable and uncertainty-aware decision support, including graph-based pose analysis and credal-set uncertainty representations [3].

The public FST.ai 2.0 record reports headline figures including an 85% reduction in review time and 93% referee trust [3]. During the present evidence audit, however, additional passages in the same public record were found to contain different timing descriptions and differently framed acceptance/trust quantities. Because the raw observations, denominators, and survey wording have not yet been reconstructed in the present open evidence package, those numbers are treated here as **prior published claims**, not as new empirical findings of this study.

The closest independent Taekwondo comparison is the 2025 feasibility study of Paris 2024 Olympic video review [8]. That study analyzed 241 cases, found κ = 0.897 agreement between AI-generated judgments and international video-review referees, and reported that the nine discrepant cases were concentrated in minimal-contact and occluded scenarios. This is relevant to FST because it suggests that the operationally hard cases may be a small but structurally distinct subset.

### 2.3 Selective prediction, abstention, and human deferral

The present study differs from the existing FST and Taekwondo video-review literature in the object being evaluated:

| Work | Primary object | Main reported evidence | Uncertainty / deferral role | Limitation relative to this study |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FST.ai [2] | AI-assisted officiating architecture | system design and operational examples | implicit reliability support | not an independent event-level uncertainty benchmark |
| FST.ai 2.0 [3] | explainable, uncertainty-aware FST ecosystem | prior-public latency/trust/accuracy descriptions | credal-set uncertainty proposed | headline observations require source-level reconciliation for SSAC reuse |
| Zhang et al. [8] | AI vs. IVR referees on Paris 2024 cases | 241 reviews, κ agreement, discrepancy and timing analysis | difficult cases discussed; human oversight retained | different model/task and no FST epistemic risk-coverage analysis |
| **Present study** | evidence validity and human-defer benchmark for FST | 546-candidate audit, provenance, cluster-aware descriptive inference | explicit separation of human confidence from model uncertainty; registered risk-coverage benchmark | frozen model/event-census stage still required for full FST efficacy estimates |

The comparison is intentionally descriptive rather than a ranking. The novelty of the present work lies in making the evidence boundary and decision-to-defer evaluation explicit and reproducible.

Selective classification formalizes the choice to abstain on uncertain inputs. El-Yaniv and Wiener define the risk-coverage trade-off: decreasing coverage can reduce predictive risk when the model can rank cases by reliability [4]. In an officiating context, this has a direct operational interpretation. A model need not make an autonomous call on every event; instead, it can support the official on well-supported cases and defer ambiguous cases for human review.

Learning-to-defer methods extend this idea by explicitly considering the downstream decision maker [5]. Bondi et al. show that human behavior can change depending on how a model's deferral is communicated, demonstrating that evaluation should address the composite human-AI system rather than the model alone [6].

This is especially important when confidence is affected by distribution shift. Ovadia et al. demonstrate that predictive uncertainty methods can fail under shift and that post-hoc calibration alone does not guarantee reliable uncertainty [7]. Tournament footage naturally contains potential shifts in camera angle, lighting, athlete morphology, equipment, movement style, competition stage, and broadcast processing. Therefore, an FST uncertainty score must be evaluated empirically rather than inferred from a generic confidence output.

## 3. FST Evaluation Scope and Human-in-the-Loop Principle

This study is restricted to the FST.ai / FST.ai 2.0 officiating research line. Broader sport-intelligence platforms and unrelated modules are outside scope.

The evaluation principle is:

\[
\text{visual evidence} \rightarrow \text{FST evidence/recommendation} \rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{support human decision}, & \text{if evidence is sufficient} \\
\text{defer to human review}, & \text{if evidence is insufficient}.
\end{cases}
\]

The system is therefore evaluated as **decision support**, not as a replacement referee.

This leads to four provenance layers:

1. **Ground truth/reference evidence:** independently adjudicated event information.
2. **Model output:** generated by a frozen FST implementation without access to reference labels.
3. **Independent context annotation:** visibility, occlusion, difficulty, contact quality, or similar evidence conditions.
4. **Derived variables:** TP, FP, FN, error, coverage, risk, latency change, or other quantities computed only after the first layers are frozen.

The central methodological rule is that **human confidence is not model uncertainty**. A human annotation of “High” or “Medium” certainty describes the annotator or evidence state; it does not establish an epistemic uncertainty measure generated by FST.

## 4. Data and Evidence Audit

### 4.1 Source candidate table

The source evidence consists of 546 candidate-level records associated with 74 recorded match identifiers. Each record contains:

- match identifier;
- clip name;
- human candidate-validation label: True Positive or False Positive;
- human confidence: High or Medium;
- difficulty: Easy, Medium, or Hard;
- action label: turning head kick, non-turning head kick, or uncertain;
- contact annotation: clear, light, or uncertain;
- visibility: good, moderate, or partial occlusion.

The annotation manual defines **True Positive** as a system-detected valid head kick and **False Positive** as a detected head kick that is not a real head kick. This wording indicates that the table is a validation set of candidate detections rather than an independently enumerated census of all true events.

That distinction is decisive. A candidate-only table contains TP and FP among proposed detections but does not reveal how many genuine actions the model failed to propose. Without an independently enumerated false-negative population, recall and F1 cannot be recovered.

### 4.2 Immutable raw layer and audit corrections

The raw table was preserved. Corrections were represented through explicit audit fields rather than destructive editing.

The audit found:

- one exact duplicate match/clip key: '624-506 / clip_001.mp4';
- a source note indicating that match identifiers '624-548' and '624-549' refer to the same bout;
- a note stating that '624-546' does not exist immediately before records labelled '624-546';
- a later note stating that '624-531' does not exist despite earlier records for '624-531';
- repeated heading typo 'MATH' instead of 'MATCH' in part of the 625-series section.

Only the exact repeated record was excluded from the canonical candidate population. The contradictory match-number notes were retained with provenance flags rather than resolved by assumption. The two IDs explicitly described as one bout were mapped to a shared clustering identifier for dependence-aware inference.

This yields 545 canonical candidate records and 73 bout-level clusters.

### 4.3 Privacy-preserving release

Athlete names were removed from the public derived candidate tables. The released files retain only the information needed to reproduce the audit and its numerical results. Raw competition video is not redistributed because the authors' ownership of FST does not automatically establish redistribution rights for event footage, broadcasts, athlete likenesses, or other third-party material.

### 4.4 Evidence roles

The fields are interpreted as follows:

- **TP/FP label:** human validation of a selected candidate event;
- **human confidence:** human/reference confidence;
- **difficulty, action type, contact, visibility:** contextual/reference annotations;
- **candidate-confirmation fraction:** descriptive property of the selected candidate population;
- **model uncertainty:** not present in this annotation table and must come from the frozen FST implementation.

This prevents a common form of evidential leakage in which variables that were assigned after seeing the outcome are later presented as independent predictors of that outcome.

## 5. Statistical Analysis

### 5.1 Candidate-confirmation fraction

For the canonical candidate set, we define

\[
q = \frac{N_{\mathrm{TP}}}{N_{\mathrm{TP}} + N_{\mathrm{FP}}}.
\]

We call \(q\) the **candidate-confirmation fraction**, not accuracy, recall, or F1. The terminology is intentional: the denominator contains selected candidate detections, not all positive and negative opportunities in the underlying bouts.

### 5.2 Bout-cluster jackknife interval

Candidate clips from the same bout are not assumed independent. Let \(G\) denote the number of bout clusters and \(q_{(-g)}\) the candidate-confirmation estimate after omitting cluster \(g\). The delete-one-cluster jackknife standard error is

\[
SE_J =
\sqrt{
\frac{G-1}{G}
\sum_{g=1}^{G}
\left(q_{(-g)}-\bar q_{(-\cdot)}\right)^2
}.
\]

A normal 95% interval is reported as

\[
q \pm 1.96 SE_J.
\]

This interval addresses within-bout clustering in the descriptive candidate population; it does not convert the sample into a complete detection benchmark.

### 5.3 Annotation-structure audit

We count marginal distributions and the frequency of each joint pattern across:

\[
(\text{label},\text{human confidence},\text{difficulty},
\text{event type},\text{contact},\text{visibility}).
\]

The purpose is not to fit a predictive model from these fields. Instead, the analysis asks whether the metadata vary sufficiently independently to support an interpretation such as “errors rise under low confidence” or “occlusion predicts model uncertainty.” If the fields are nearly deterministic functions of the outcome label, such an interpretation would be circular.

### 5.4 Stratified descriptive analysis

Candidate-confirmation fractions are also tabulated by visibility, difficulty, human confidence, event label, and contact quality. These are explicitly descriptive because the annotation fields are structurally coupled.

No null-hypothesis test is used to turn this coupling into a causal claim. Effect sizes, denominators, and provenance are more informative here than a p-value.

### 5.5 Registered event-level benchmark

The public repository also defines the stronger evaluation required for FST efficacy.

An independent file 'ground_truth_events.csv' should enumerate all eligible reference events. A separate 'fst_ssac_predictions.csv' should contain all predictions from a frozen FST configuration. A deterministic one-to-one temporal matcher then derives:

\[
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \quad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},
\]

and

\[
F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P+R}.
\]

The event-matching tolerance, class rule, model version, configuration fingerprint, and time base must be declared in advance. Sensitivity analyses should repeat evaluation under alternative defensible temporal tolerances.

The current paper does not fabricate those files and therefore does not report event-level recall or F1.

### 5.6 Registered uncertainty/selective-prediction benchmark

Let \(U(x)\) denote a numerical model-generated uncertainty score, with higher values meaning greater uncertainty. For threshold \(\tau\),

\[
C(\tau)=
\frac{1}{n}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\mathbf{1}\{U(x_i)\le \tau\}
\]

is the **coverage**, and

\[
R(\tau)=
P(\text{error}\mid U(x)\le\tau)
\]

is the **selective risk** among retained AI-supported decisions.

A reliable deferral mechanism should reduce retained error as coverage decreases. The preferred evaluation unit is an eligible IVR decision/request if the frozen implementation emits an uncertainty value for every request. If numerical uncertainty exists only for emitted FST candidates, the curve has a narrower interpretation: coverage is the fraction of emitted candidates retained and risk is FP/(TP+FP) among those retained candidates. Such a candidate-level curve does not measure false-negative deferral because missed reference events have no emitted candidate score. This distinction is prespecified in the repository's selective-prediction protocol.

Threshold selection for a headline test result must be fixed outside the final test population. The FST 2.0 uncertainty analysis will use only uncertainty generated by the frozen implementation; human High/Medium confidence is explicitly excluded.

### 6.4 Visibility and difficulty strata

After exact deduplication, the visibility strata are:

**Table 4. Descriptive candidate-confirmation by visibility**

| Visibility | n | TP-labelled | FP-labelled | Candidate-confirmation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Good | 171 | 171 | 0 | 100.00% |
| Moderate | 152 | 152 | 0 | 100.00% |
| Partial occlusion | 222 | 126 | 96 | 56.76% |

The same numerical split appears for difficulty because the source annotations couple Easy with good visibility, Medium with moderate visibility, and Hard with the two partial-occlusion patterns.

The partial-occlusion fraction is therefore descriptively important but should not be read as a causal estimate of the effect of occlusion. It is equally consistent with an annotation workflow in which difficult/uncertain labels were assigned after candidate correctness was known.

### 6.5 Human confidence is not model uncertainty

Among canonical candidates:

- High human confidence: 293 TP, 0 FP;
- Medium human confidence: 156 TP, 96 FP.

It would be tempting to claim that lower confidence predicts errors. The audit shows why that claim is not yet scientifically justified: human confidence is embedded in the same near-deterministic annotation pattern as the TP/FP label.

The result is therefore not “FST uncertainty is calibrated.” The correct result is:

> the current human annotations encode a strong relationship between evidence difficulty and candidate correctness, but their construction is too coupled to serve as independent validation of FST epistemic uncertainty.

This distinction is the principal evidential finding of the study.

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 The central result is an evidence-boundary result

The numerical candidate-confirmation fraction of 82.39% is useful, but it is not the most important finding. The more consequential result is that the annotation table contains strong structural dependence that would make several superficially attractive analyses scientifically misleading.

For example, reporting 100% candidate confirmation for “good visibility” and 56.76% for “partial occlusion” without describing the annotation construction would suggest a clean robustness experiment. The joint-pattern audit shows that this interpretation is not warranted. Visibility, difficulty, human confidence, event ambiguity, contact ambiguity, and TP/FP labels are nearly locked together.

This is precisely the kind of issue that can occur when sports-AI systems evolve from prototype demonstrations into research datasets. Operational annotations may be perfectly adequate for reviewing individual candidate clips, yet unsuitable for estimating generalization, calibration, or uncertainty unless their provenance is reconstructed.

### 7.2 Why false negatives change the scientific question

A detector-validation table answers:

> “Of the candidate events surfaced for review, how many were confirmed?”

It does not answer:

> “Of all true head-kick events in the eligible population, how many did FST detect?”

The first question has denominator \(TP+FP\). The second requires \(TP+FN\).

This is why the paper does not transform the 82.39% candidate-confirmation fraction into a standard object-detection accuracy statement. A system can have high precision and poor recall, or vice versa. In officiating, missed critical events may be at least as consequential as false alerts, so FN must be directly observable.

### 7.3 From confidence to selective decision support

The practical value of uncertainty in officiating is not a better-looking confidence bar. It is a decision rule.

If FST can rank events by epistemic uncertainty, then the operational question becomes whether high-uncertainty events are enriched for errors and whether deferring them reduces retained risk at an acceptable loss of coverage. This is the selective-classification perspective [4].

For a federation or tournament operator, the relevant operating point is not necessarily maximum coverage. A more useful target may be:

- high coverage for routine well-supported evidence;
- automatic escalation of uncertain cases;
- visible evidence overlays for the official;
- no autonomous override of the authorized referee/jury;
- a fully logged decision trail.

This is also consistent with human-AI evidence showing that deferral itself influences human judgment and must be evaluated as part of the combined decision system [6].

### 7.4 Relation to the Paris Olympic video-review study

The Paris 2024 feasibility study [8] reported nine discrepancies among 241 reviewed cases and observed that disagreement concentrated in minimal-contact or visually occluded situations. The present FST audit independently points to the same operational challenge: the false candidate labels are concentrated entirely in the difficult/ambiguous/occluded annotation state.

The two studies should not be numerically compared as if they used the same task, sampling frame, model, or ground truth. They do, however, converge on a design implication: **the long tail of ambiguous visual evidence should be a primary evaluation target rather than a residual error category.**

This is where FST's uncertainty-aware research direction can add value. The next benchmark should not merely ask whether FST matches a referee on average. It should ask whether model-generated epistemic uncertainty identifies the cases where human review is most valuable.

### 7.5 Implications for sports organizations

The audit suggests five implementation principles for AI-assisted officiating.

First, **preserve raw evidence and provenance**. Corrections should be logged, not silently rewritten.

Second, **separate model outputs from human labels**. A post-hoc human confidence rating is not a model uncertainty score.

Third, **evaluate at the decision-relevant unit**. Frames and clips are nested within bouts; treating every clip as IID creates pseudo-replication.

Fourth, **measure deferral performance**. Federations need to know the coverage-risk trade-off, not only top-line classification accuracy.

Fifth, **keep the human authority explicit**. AI should expose evidence and uncertainty while the official retains responsibility for the final competition decision.

These principles generalize beyond Taekwondo to sports where video review combines brief visual evidence with rule-based adjudication.

## 9. Limitations

This study has six principal limitations.

**First**, the source population is candidate based. It does not independently enumerate all true head-kick events and therefore cannot support recall or F1.

**Second**, the original annotation procedure is not documented in sufficient detail to establish that confidence, difficulty, contact, and visibility were assigned independently of candidate correctness. Their near-deterministic coupling therefore limits inferential interpretation.

**Third**, the candidate-confirmation fraction is conditional on the mechanism that generated the candidate set. It should not be generalized to all tournament actions without knowing that sampling mechanism.

**Fourth**, the present paper does not claim a new controlled review-latency effect or referee-trust effect. Prior FST.ai 2.0 publications contain such claims, but their underlying observations and definitions are being reconciled separately.

**Fifth**, model-generated FST epistemic uncertainty is not present in the released candidate table. The selective-prediction analysis is therefore a registered evaluation protocol rather than a result of the present audit.

**Sixth**, raw competition video is subject to third-party rights and privacy constraints. A future open full-video benchmark must resolve those rights or use a scientifically equivalent, legally releasable representation.

These limitations narrow the claims but strengthen their validity. The objective of the present study is to establish exactly what the available evidence supports and to prevent unsupported performance statements from entering a high-stakes officiating evaluation.

## 10. Future Evaluation

The next FST benchmark should freeze three independent artifacts:

1. an event-level ground-truth census;
2. an FST/FST 2.0 prediction export generated without reference-label access;
3. a numerical model uncertainty/evidence output.

The benchmark should then report:

- TP, FP, FN;
- precision, recall, F1 with bout-clustered intervals;
- turning vs non-turning performance;
- temporal localization error;
- performance under good, moderate, and occluded visibility;
- calibration and/or ranking quality of model uncertainty;
- risk-coverage curves and area under the risk-coverage curve;
- characteristics of deferred cases;
- paired end-to-end review latency if a comparable baseline is available.

A prespecified threshold should be selected on validation data, not the final test set. The most informative outcome would be evidence that FST can maintain low selective risk at useful coverage while sending ambiguous cases to the human official.

---

## 11. Conclusion

AI-assisted officiating should not be judged only by how often an algorithm produces a correct answer. In high-speed, partially observed competition settings, a trustworthy system must also identify when the evidence is inadequate and preserve an explicit path to human judgment.

The audit of 546 championship-derived FST candidate records produced a canonical set of 545 candidates with an 82.39% candidate-confirmation fraction (95% bout-cluster jackknife CI: 81.16%–83.61%). More importantly, 97.99% of raw observations fall into only four joint annotation patterns, and all 96 false candidates share the same difficult, uncertain, partially occluded annotation state. This shows that the present annotation fields are strongly dependent and must not be mistaken for independent model-uncertainty evidence.

The resulting evaluation framework separates ground truth, model output, contextual annotation, and derived outcomes; refuses to report recall without false negatives; treats bout dependence explicitly; and defines the FST 2.0 uncertainty problem as selective prediction with human deferral.

For sports organizations, the practical question is therefore not “Can AI make every call?” It is “Can AI provide useful evidence, recognize when that evidence is insufficient, and hand the difficult case back to the official in a transparent and auditable way?” That is the standard against which uncertainty-aware officiating systems should be evaluated.
