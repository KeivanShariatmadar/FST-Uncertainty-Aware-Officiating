# Uncertainty-Aware AI-Assisted Video Review for Faster and More Reliable Officiating in Competitive Taekwondo

## Introduction
Instant Video Replay in Taekwondo must resolve scoring actions that occur within fractions of a second, often under occlusion, rapid body rotation, and limited camera perspective. FST.ai is a human-in-the-loop decision-support framework for head-kick review. This study asks how such a system should be evaluated when visual evidence is incomplete, and how uncertainty should determine when AI evidence is sufficiently reliable to assist an official and when the case should remain with human review.

## Methods
We audited a championship-derived FST candidate dataset containing 546 annotated candidate clips from 74 recorded match identifiers. The supplied annotation protocol defines True Positive as a system-detected valid head kick and False Positive as a system detection that is not a real head kick. Each candidate also has human confidence, difficulty, kick type, contact, and row-aligned visibility annotations. We preserved the raw records, removed only one exact duplicate match/clip entry for the canonical population, and treated a documented pair of match identifiers referring to one bout as a single clustering unit. Human annotation confidence was separated from model epistemic uncertainty. Because the candidate table does not independently enumerate missed true events, we report candidate-confirmation statistics rather than recall or F1.

## Results
The canonical audit population contains 545 candidate clips: 449 were human-validated as true events and 96 as false candidates, corresponding to an 82.39% candidate-confirmation rate. The audit exposed strong dependence among annotation fields: 535 of 546 raw records (97.99%) fall into four repeated joint patterns. All 96 false-positive-labelled candidates are Medium-confidence, Hard, uncertain-event, uncertain-contact, partial-occlusion cases; among 222 partially occluded candidates, 126 are TP-labelled and 96 FP-labelled. This structure means human confidence, difficulty, and visibility cannot be treated as independent model-uncertainty evidence. Full detection performance requires an independent event census that also observes false negatives.

## Conclusion
For AI-assisted officiating, reliability depends not only on rapid classification but on recognizing when the available evidence is insufficient for an automated recommendation. The FST evaluation therefore treats uncertainty as a decision-to-defer problem and makes provenance, deduplication, independent ground truth, and human override explicit parts of the analytics pipeline. The released de-identified data and evaluation scripts provide a reproducible basis for uncertainty-aware officiating without overstating what candidate-only annotations can establish.
