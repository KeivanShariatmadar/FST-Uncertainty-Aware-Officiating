# Uncertainty-Aware AI-Assisted Video Review for Faster and More Reliable Officiating in Competitive Taekwondo

## Introduction
Instant Video Replay in Taekwondo must resolve scoring actions that occur within fractions of a second, often under occlusion, rapid body rotation, and limited camera perspective. FST.ai is a human-in-the-loop decision-support framework for head-kick review. This study asks how such a system should be evaluated when both visual evidence and model confidence may be uncertain, and whether the evaluation itself can distinguish reliable AI evidence from cases that should remain with human officials.

## Methods
We audited a championship-derived FST candidate dataset containing 546 annotated candidate clips from 74 recorded match identifiers. Each candidate includes a human validation label together with contextual annotations for confidence, difficulty, kick type, and contact quality. We preserved the raw records, removed only one exact duplicate match/clip entry for the canonical audit population, and treated a documented pair of match identifiers referring to the same bout as one clustering unit. Human annotation confidence was explicitly separated from model epistemic uncertainty. Because the current candidate table does not independently enumerate missed true events, we report candidate-confirmation statistics rather than recall or F1. A reproducible evaluation protocol was defined for subsequent independent ground-truth/model-output matching, bout-clustered uncertainty intervals, contextual robustness analysis, and risk–coverage evaluation.

## Results
The canonical audit population contains 545 candidate clips: 449 were human-validated as true events and 96 as false candidates, corresponding to an 82.39% candidate-confirmation rate. The audit also exposed substantial dependence among annotation fields: 535 of 546 raw records (97.99%) fall into four repeated joint annotation patterns, and all 96 false-positive-labelled candidates share the same Medium-confidence/Hard/uncertain-event/uncertain-contact combination. These findings show that human confidence and difficulty labels cannot be treated as independent model-uncertainty evidence. They also demonstrate why false negatives and model-generated numerical uncertainty must be reconstructed independently before reporting full detection or selective-prediction performance.

## Conclusion
For AI-assisted officiating, reliability depends not only on fast classification but on knowing when available evidence is insufficient for an automated recommendation. The FST evaluation therefore treats uncertainty as a decision-to-defer problem and makes provenance, deduplication, independent ground truth, and human override explicit parts of the analytics pipeline. The released audit data and evaluation scripts provide a reproducible basis for testing uncertainty-aware officiating without overstating what candidate-only annotations can establish.
